# Different Minds: Verified Fact Sheet

Researched: November 10, 2026. Audience for the site: teachers, parents and classmates.

## How to use this file

- Every fact on the site must come from this file and keep its source number. If a page needs a fact that isn't here, stop and ask Lorenzo. Don't add it from memory.
- Rewrite in plain language for the audience, but don't change the numbers or what they mean.
- Show each source as a numbered citation on the page, and list it on the Sources page.
- Numbers describe groups, not individuals. Say "about," "roughly" or "an estimated" where the source does.
- Re-check this file at least once a year; prevalence numbers and definitions change.

## ADHD

### What it is and how common

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | In 2022, about 1 in 9 US children aged 3–17 (11.4%, about 7.1 million) had ever been diagnosed with ADHD; 10.5% had current ADHD. | [5] |
| A2 | About 1 million more children had ever received an ADHD diagnosis in 2022 than in 2016. | [5] |
| A3 | Among children with current ADHD, 58% had moderate or severe ADHD. | [5] |
| A4 | In 2023, an estimated 15.5 million US adults (6.0%) had a current ADHD diagnosis, and about half were diagnosed at age 18 or older. ADHD does not end in childhood. | [6] |
| A5 | About one-third of US children with ADHD received no ADHD-specific treatment in 2022. | [5], [7] |

### Diagnosis (DSM-5-TR, as summarized by the CDC)

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A6 | There are 9 inattention symptoms and 9 hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms. A diagnosis needs 6 or more in one category for children up to age 16, or 5 or more for people 17 and older. | [8] |
| A7 | Symptoms must last at least 6 months, several must have been present before age 12, they must show up in two or more settings (such as home and school), and they must clearly interfere with daily life. | [8] |
| A8 | Clinicians collect reports from parents, teachers and the child, use rating scales, and rule out other explanations. There is no single test. | [8] |
| A9 | The three presentations are mainly inattentive, mainly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined. | [8b] |

### Causes

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A10 | ADHD is strongly genetic. Across 37 twin studies, the average heritability is about 74%. Heritability describes differences across a population, not how much of one person's ADHD is "from their parents." | [10] |
| A11 | Many genes each have a very small effect; there is no single "ADHD gene." | [10] |

### The overdiagnosis question (for the Myth/Fact card)

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A12 | A 2021 review of 334 studies found evidence that ADHD is overdiagnosed and overtreated in children and adolescents, especially in milder cases, where the harms of a diagnosis may outweigh the benefits. | [11] |
| A13 | Other experts disagree with that conclusion and think ADHD is still underdiagnosed and undertreated in many groups. The review's authors say underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis are also important issues. | [11b] |

Suggested card: "ADHD is overdiagnosed." Answer: **It's complicated.** Both overdiagnosis and underdiagnosis happen, in different groups. Cite A12, A13 and A5.

### What helps

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A14 | For preschoolers (ages 4–5), the first treatment the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends is parent training in behavior management and/or behavioral classroom interventions. | [9b] |
| A15 | For ages 6–11, the AAP recommends FDA-approved medication along with parent training and/or classroom behavioral interventions (preferably both). For teens, it recommends medication with the teen's agreement, plus behavioral support when available. | [9b] |
| A16 | The AAP says school supports (classroom placement, instructional and behavioral supports, often an IEP or 504 plan) are a necessary part of any treatment plan. | [9b] |

Don't give medical advice or name specific medications on the site. Point readers to their doctor.

### Executive function (for the Executive Function page)

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A17 | Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes three core executive function skills: working memory (holding information in mind and using it), inhibitory control (resisting impulses and distractions, pausing before acting) and cognitive flexibility (switching gears as demands change). | [13] |
| A18 | The Center compares executive function to an air traffic control system that manages many planes at once. | [13] |

## Dyslexia

### What it is and how common

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D1 | In October 2025, the International Dyslexia Association (IDA) updated its definition for the first time since 2002. It defines dyslexia as a specific learning disability with difficulties in word reading and/or spelling (accuracy, speed or both) that vary by writing system. | [1], [1b] |
| D2 | Under the 2025 definition, the difficulties fall on a continuum of severity and persist even with instruction that works for the person's peers. | [1] |
| D3 | The 2025 definition removed the old link to IQ ("unexpected in relation to other cognitive abilities"). Dyslexia occurs at all levels of intelligence. | [1], [3] |
| D4 | Difficulties with the sounds of language (phonological processing) and word parts (morphology) are common but not universal. Weak early oral language often comes before reading problems. | [1] |
| D5 | A 2022 meta-analysis estimated that about 7% of primary-school children worldwide have dyslexia (7.10%), with higher rates in boys (9.2%) than girls (4.7%). Estimates vary with how dyslexia is defined. | [14] |
| D6 | Dyslexia rates were similar across writing systems, including alphabetic scripts and Chinese. Dyslexia is not an English-only condition. | [14], [1] |
| D7 | How dyslexia shows up depends on the writing system. In more regular spelling systems (such as Italian or German), it often shows up more as slow reading than as inaccurate reading. | [1b], [3] |
| D8 | Dyslexia is lifelong; children do not outgrow it. With proper help, many people with dyslexia learn to read and write well. | [3], [9] |

### Causes

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D9 | Causes are multifactorial: genetic, neurobiological and environmental influences interacting over development. | [1] |
| D10 | Dyslexia runs in families. Twin studies estimate heritability at about 40–70%, and many genes each contribute a small effect. | [15], [16] |

### Effects

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D11 | The 2025 definition recognizes possible secondary effects on reading comprehension, academic achievement, psychological well-being (including anxiety, low self-esteem and depression) and employment. | [1] |
| D12 | Students with dyslexia often end up feeling "dumb" and less capable than they actually are. | [9] |

### What helps

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D13 | The strongest evidence supports explicit, systematic teaching of letter-sound relationships (phonics), decoding and word recognition, plus practice reading connected text. This kind of teaching helps all readers, not just those with dyslexia. | [18], [19] |
| D14 | Students with dyslexia often need a lot of structured practice with immediate, corrective feedback. | [9] |
| D15 | Common accommodations include extra time, help with note-taking, modified assignments, audiobooks, text-to-speech and word-processing tools, and alternative ways to show learning. | [9], [20] |
| D16 | Early identification and support are especially effective, but support helps at any age. | [1] |

## Dyscalculia

### What it is and how common

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1 | Dyscalculia is a persistent difficulty with processing numbers and quantities that isn't explained by low intelligence or inadequate schooling. It often starts with basic number sense in the preschool years. | [21] |
| C2 | About 3–7% of children, adolescents and adults have dyscalculia. Estimates vary with the criteria used. | [21], [22] |
| C3 | Unlike dyslexia, the sex ratio is roughly even. | [21] |
| C4 | Dyscalculia is less understood and less often diagnosed than dyslexia, and there are no agreed-on diagnostic criteria among researchers. | [22] |

### Dyscalculia vs math anxiety (for the Myth/Fact card)

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C5 | In a study of 1,757 UK students, children with dyscalculia were about twice as likely to have high math anxiety, but about 77% of highly math-anxious children had typical or high math performance. The two are different problems that need different help. | [23] |

### What helps

| ID | Fact | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C6 | The US Institute of Education Sciences recommends six practices for students struggling with math: systematic instruction, clear math language, concrete and visual representations, number lines, explicit word-problem instruction, and regular fluency practice. | [24] |
| C7 | Concrete representations (blocks, fraction tiles) and semi-concrete ones (drawings, strip diagrams, number lines) help connect numbers to quantities. | [24] |

## Claims to avoid (not supported by the sources)

- Dyslexia means seeing letters backwards or letters moving on the page (see S1).
- Colored overlays, tinted lenses or eye exercises treat dyslexia (see S1).
- A "dyslexic advantage" or ADHD "superpower" as a general rule. Some people have real strengths, but the research is mixed. Share individual stories from Voices instead.
- Specific medication names or doses.
- Any statistic without a source number from this file.
